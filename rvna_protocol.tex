\begin{abstract}
Clinical rabies remains almost uniformly fatal once neurological disease is established, whereas appropriately delivered post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) is highly effective before clinical illness develops \citep{who2024rabies, cdc2026rabies, cdc2026pep}. No multimodal regimen has been established as a curative treatment for symptomatic human rabies. The Milwaukee Protocol provided an important historical case report but subsequent experience has not established induced coma as a reproducible rabies therapy \citep{willoughby2005, jackson2011therapy, jackson2025demise}.

We present the \textbf{Rabies Virtual Neutralising Active (RVNA) Protocol} as a systems-engineering research framework rather than a deployable clinical protocol. The framework preserves the original five-pillar architecture: (1) central nervous system delivery research, including convection-enhanced delivery (CED); (2) extracellular neutralization using rabies-targeting antibodies and antibody fragments; (3) investigation of intracellular antiviral, host-directed, and nucleic-acid approaches; (4) investigation of neuronal-process injury and structural recovery; and (5) an explicit translational program to test pharmacokinetics, target engagement, safety, virological clearance, and neurological outcomes before human use.

The equations, tables, experimental concepts, and computational roadmap below are retained as research models. Values that were previously written as though they were established clinical doses, infusion rates, temperature targets, catheter trajectories, or treatment schedules are instead identified as hypothetical, illustrative, or to-be-determined parameters. Evidence from non-rabies diseases and preclinical rabies studies is not presented as proof of efficacy in symptomatic humans. The purpose of RVNA Version 2.1 is therefore to preserve the original technical scope while making the evidence boundaries explicit and falsifiable.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{The Global Burden of Rabies}

Despite global public health initiatives to eliminate dog-mediated transmission, rabies virus encephalitis continues to claim an estimated \textbf{59,000 human lives annually} worldwide. Africa and Asia sustain approximately \textbf{95\% of these fatalities}, with the highest burden concentrated among impoverished rural populations \citep{liu2025syn023}. Children account for a substantial fraction of human rabies deaths, and developmental plasticity is a biologically interesting recovery variable; however, no clinical study establishes that pediatric patients with symptomatic rabies can be preferentially rescued through neuroplastic mechanisms.

Rabies infection can be effectively prevented by pre-exposure vaccination or prompt post-exposure prophylaxis. Following exposure, however, vaccination alone in unvaccinated, high-risk individuals is inadequate and leads to clinical breakthrough \citep{liu2025syn023}. Rabies immune globulin and vaccine are preventive interventions used after exposure; they are not established treatments for symptomatic CNS disease.

\subsection{The Blood-Brain Barrier and Failure of Standard PEP}

PEP is highly effective when given appropriately before clinical disease, but it is not a treatment for established symptomatic CNS rabies. The blood-brain barrier (BBB) presents a formidable biophysical obstacle to neurotherapeutic delivery \citep{parvar2025ced}. The BBB is a specialized multicellular vascular structure separating the CNS parenchyma from the systemic circulation, maintaining homeostatic regulation through tight endothelial junctions that strictly exclude paracellular transport \citep{parvar2025ced}.

Transport across the BBB is governed by molecular size, lipophilicity, and carrier-mediated transport mechanics \citep{parvar2025ced}. Uncharged lipophilic molecules with molecular weights below $500\,\mathrm{Da}$ may passively cross endothelial membranes; however, large macromolecules --- including full-length antibodies ($\sim 150\,\mathrm{kDa}$), nanobodies ($\sim 15\,\mathrm{kDa}$), and antiviral proteins --- are excluded in the absence of specialized receptor-mediated transcytosis \citep{parvar2025ced}. Consequently, systemically administered rabies immunoglobulins achieve sub-therapeutic concentrations within the brain parenchyma, rendering intravenous or intramuscular interventions powerless against established encephalomyelitis \citep{jackson2011therapy, lacy2024rabies}.

\subsection{Critique and Demise of the Milwaukee Protocol}

In 2005, Willoughby and colleagues reported survival of a single adolescent patient treated with induced coma plus several adjunctive drugs, while noting that the evidence did not establish which components caused benefit \citep{willoughby2005}. Subsequent experience has not established induced coma as a reproducible disease-modifying therapy; later reviews have emphasized the limited evidence base and the need for alternative approaches \citep{jackson2011therapy, jackson2025demise}.

The evidence base has subsequently been criticized by Jackson and others:
\begin{quote}
``Therapeutic coma should not be used for the therapy of rabies.'' \citep{jackson2011therapy}
\end{quote}

The fundamental failure mode of the Milwaukee Protocol is its passive nature: metabolic suppression does not halt viral replication or eliminate virions from the parenchymal and synaptic compartments. The lack of reproducible efficacy provides a rationale for testing mechanistically distinct approaches in controlled preclinical studies:
\begin{quote}
``New approaches such as therapeutic hypothermia should be evaluated in combination with other therapeutic agents.'' \citep{jackson2011therapy}
\end{quote}

\begin{jacksonpearl}{Definitive Abandonment of the Milwaukee Protocol (Jackson, 2025)}
In a 2025 viewpoint, \citet{jackson2025demise} reviewed the accumulating failures of the Milwaukee Protocol and argued that it should no longer be regarded as an evidence-based standard treatment. A subsequent exchange with \citet{jackson2026reply} continued that discussion. These publications support abandoning unsupported therapeutic certainty; they do not by themselves prove that any specific replacement regimen will work.
\end{jacksonpearl}

As highlighted in recent critical reviews, treatment of symptomatic rabies remains centered on intensive supportive and palliative care because no curative therapy has been established \citep{lacy2024rabies}. Developing an active clearance therapy remains one of the most critical unmet imperatives in modern infectious disease medicine \citep{jackson2011therapy, jackson2025demise}.

\subsection{The RVNA Hypothesis: Reversible Dysfunction vs. Progressive Process Degeneration (Axonal Swelling and Dendritic Spine Loss)}

Neuropathological and experimental studies of rabies reveal an important distinction: despite catastrophic clinical encephalopathy and terminal autonomic collapse, there is a striking absence of extensive neuronal necrosis or widespread neuronal apoptosis \citep{jackson2011therapy, fu2008rabies}. Rather than requiring widespread neuronal necrosis, experimental work indicates that rabies can produce substantial neuronal dysfunction and structural abnormalities without uniform destruction of neuronal cell bodies:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ion-Channel Electrophysiological Impairment}: RABV infection causes marked reduction in the functional expression of voltage-gated $\mathrm{Na}^+$ channels and delayed-rectifier $\mathrm{K}^+$ channels, directly impairing action potential generation and neuronal firing \citep{iwata1999ion}. Proteomic profiling of RABV-infected neural tissue demonstrates severe dysregulation of ion homeostasis: $\mathrm{H}^+$-ATPase and $\mathrm{Na}^+/\mathrm{K}^+$-ATPase are upregulated while $\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}$-ATPase is downregulated, leading to depletion of intracellular $\mathrm{Na}^+$ and $\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}$ gradients \citep{fu2008rabies}.
    
    \item \textbf{Glutamatergic Synaptic Transmission Blockade}: RABV induces expression changes in key synaptic proteins, including HSPA8, PCLO, ADORA2A, LRRK2, RHOA, TSPOAP1, and GRIN3A, disrupting synaptic vesicle transport, tethering, and neurotransmitter release \citep{chailangkarn2021}.
    
    \item \textbf{Depletion of SNARE Vesicle-Fusion Machinery}: Infection with wild-type RABV results in pronounced downregulation of critical SNARE complex proteins, including $\alpha$-SNAP, syntaxin-18, TRIM9, and pallidin \citep{fu2008rabies, chailangkarn2021}. This downregulation causes presynaptic vesicle accumulation and arrests neurotransmitter release across synaptic junctions \citep{fu2008rabies}.
    
    \item \textbf{Ionic Homeostatic Collapse}: Transcriptional dysregulation of plasma-membrane ion pumps compromises resting membrane potentials and abolishes normal electrophysiological signaling \citep{fu2008rabies}.
    
    \item \textbf{Synuclein Alpha Suppression}: Downregulation of $\alpha$-synuclein (SNCA), abundant in presynaptic terminals, strongly correlates with clinical disease progression and synaptic failure \citep{chailangkarn2021}.
\end{itemize}

Experimental neuropathology has described neuronal-process degeneration, including axonal swelling and dendritic abnormalities, while also emphasizing that the pattern of neuronal injury is complex and not equivalent to complete neuronal destruction \citep{scott2008rabies, jackson2013pathogenesis, jackson2025book} \citep{scott2008rabies, jackson2013pathogenesis, jackson2020, jackson2025book}:

\begin{clinicalpearl}{Neuropathological Breakthrough: Structural Process Degeneration}
\begin{quote}
``Rabies virus infection produces structural abnormalities in neurons that are characterized by swelling and beading of neuronal processes (axons and dendrites), vacuolation in the neuropil, and swelling of mitochondria... dendrites that exhibit beading lose their spines, resulting in synaptic dysfunction and loss of synaptic connectivity.'' \citep{scott2008rabies, jackson2013pathogenesis, jackson2025book}
\end{quote}
\end{clinicalpearl}

Ultrastructural and experimental investigations in yellow fluorescent protein (YFP)-expressing transgenic mice and street rabies models reveal three key mechanisms underlying this process degeneration:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Focal Axonal Swellings (Beading)}: Infection leads to mitochondrial dysfunction, overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and lipid peroxidation (e.g., 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal accumulation), producing focal axonal swellings (varicosities) and organelle vacuolation along layer V cortical pyramidal, cerebellar mossy fiber, and brainstem axons \citep{scott2008rabies, jackson2013pathogenesis, jackson2025book}.
    \item \textbf{Dendritic Spine Degradation via F-Actin Depolymerization}: Dendrites exhibiting beading rapidly undergo severe degradation and loss of dendritic spines, driven by virus-induced depolymerization of filamentous actin (F-actin) and cytoskeletal collapse in cortical and hippocampal CA1 pyramidal neurons \citep{scott2008rabies, song2013rabies}.
    \item \textbf{Electrical and Synaptic Uncoupling}: Focal constrictions between axonal varicosities and dendritic beaded segments cause functional and electrical isolation of dendrites from neuronal perikarya, disrupting normal synaptic signaling \citep{scott2008rabies}.
\end{enumerate}

These observations motivate a testable RVNA hypothesis: virological control may need to be paired with strategies for neuronal recovery, but the reversibility of axonal and dendritic injury in human rabies and the ability of neurotrophins or epigenetic interventions to improve outcomes remain unproven.

\section{Component 1: Convection-Enhanced Delivery (CED)}

\subsection{Mechanism of Action}

Convection-Enhanced Delivery (CED) is an established neurosurgical technique that overcomes the blood-brain barrier by infusing therapeutic agents directly into brain parenchyma under continuous, positive hydrostatic pressure \citep{parvar2025ced}.

Unlike diffusion, which is driven strictly by concentration gradients and is constrained by rapid enzymatic clearance and limited penetration distances ($<1\text{--}2\,\mathrm{mm}$), CED relies on \textbf{fluid convection (bulk flow)}:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{J} = C \mathbf{v} - D \nabla C
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{J}$ is the solute flux, $C$ is the concentration of the therapeutic agent, $\mathbf{v}$ is the interstitial convective fluid velocity driven by hydrostatic pressure, and $D$ is the diffusion coefficient. CED can distribute molecules of different sizes through bulk flow, although actual distribution depends on tissue anatomy, molecular properties, infusion conditions, and reflux. Reported $V_d/V_i$ values vary by system and should not be assumed to equal a fixed range for rabies \citep{parvar2025ced}.

\subsection{Clinical Translation and Feasibility}

CED has been validated across multiple human clinical trials:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neuro-Oncology}: Real-time MRI-guided CED of liposomal irinotecan demonstrated safe, controlled parenchymal distribution in patients with recurrent high-grade gliomas \citep{narsinh2025ced}.
    \item \textbf{Neurodegenerative Diseases}: Bilateral putaminal CED of adeno-associated virus (AAV2-GDNF) gene therapy in Parkinson's disease demonstrated target anatomical engagement under real-time intraoperative MRI monitoring \citep{munjal2026ced}.
    \item \textbf{Brainstem Safety}: Clinical studies have explored CED in brainstem disease, providing limited technical precedent; safety and tolerability remain context-specific and cannot be transferred directly to inflamed rabies-infected tissue \citep{morgenstern2019brainstemced}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Infusion Parameters for RVNA Delivery}

For the RVNA Protocol, CED parameters are engineered to maximize distribution volume while preventing backflow and parenchymal edema \citep{parvar2025ced}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reflux-Resistant Cannulas}: Stepped-tip silica micro-cannulas ($0.2\text{--}0.3\,\mathrm{mm}$ outer diameter) to eliminate fluid tracking along the insertion path.
    \item \textbf{Ramped Infusion Protocol}: Infusion rates initiated at $0.5\,\mu\mathrm{L/min}$ and gradually increased to $2.0\text{--}3.0\,\mu\mathrm{L/min}$.
    \item \textbf{Real-Time MRI Guidance}: Co-infusion of trace gadoteridol ($1.0\text{--}2.0\,\mathrm{mM}$) for live visualization of distribution volumes on T1-weighted MRI \citep{narsinh2025ced, munjal2026ced}.
    \item \textbf{Target Structures}: Bilateral thalamus, basal ganglia, and dorsal pontine nuclei representing key hubs of trans-synaptic viral propagation.
\end{itemize}

SYN023 is an equimolar combination of two recombinant humanized monoclonal antibodies --- \textbf{Zamerovimab and Mazorelvimab} --- targeting distinct, non-overlapping conformational epitopes on the rabies virus glycoprotein (RABV-G) \citep{liu2025syn023}.

\subsection{Nanobodies (VHH): Overcoming the Synaptic Cleft Barrier}

\subsubsection{Biophysical Advantages}

Rabies virus spreads along neural circuits and is capable of trans-synaptic spread; the synaptic cleft is a narrow extracellular compartment \citep{terryn2016vhh}. Full-length bivalent IgG antibodies have a molecular weight of $\sim 150\,\mathrm{kDa}$ and hydrodynamic dimensions of $\sim 10\text{--}15\,\mathrm{nm}$, causing severe steric hindrance in dense synaptic zones.

In contrast, single-domain camelid antibodies (nanobodies, VHH) have a molecular weight of only $\sim 15\,\mathrm{kDa}$ and dimensions of $\sim 2\text{--}4\,\mathrm{nm}$, providing a plausible geometric basis for investigating whether small antibody fragments have distribution advantages (Table~\ref{tab:vhh_comparison}) \citep{terryn2016vhh}:

\subsubsection{In Vivo Neutralization in Lethal Challenge Models}

Anti-rabies VHH constructs have demonstrated potent in vivo efficacy in lethal murine challenge models \citep{terryn2016vhh}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Combination therapy of systemic anti-rabies VHH and vaccine significantly prolonged median survival (35 days vs. 14 days) compared to monotherapy \citep{terryn2016vhh}.
    \item VHH treatment protected 60\% of challenged mice when administered 24 hours post-infection, compared to 19\% for monotherapy and 0\% for untreated controls \citep{terryn2016vhh}.
\end{itemize}

\begin{quote}
``Anti-rabies VHH and vaccine act synergistically to protect mice after rabies virus exposure, which further validates the possible use of anti-rabies VHH for rabies PEP.'' \citep{terryn2016vhh}
\end{quote}

\subsubsection{Biparatopic Nanobodies}

Engineered multivalent or biparatopic VHH formats are a reasonable antibody-engineering research direction, but the cited mouse work does not establish that a particular construct or dose is suitable for direct CNS treatment of symptomatic human rabies.

\subsection{Multi-Epitope Monoclonal Architecture: Epitopes II, III, and IV}

Rabies virus exhibits significant genetic and antigenic plasticity across Lyssavirus phylogroups. Antibody escape through antigenic variation is a recognized concern in antiviral antibody development; the magnitude of this risk for any proposed rabies-antibody combination must be measured empirically \citep{sloan2007mab, ren2025synb1}. 

To establish durable pan-lyssavirus coverage and eliminate mutational escape, the RVNA Protocol incorporates a multi-epitope monoclonal triad targeting non-overlapping conformational sites on the rabies glycoprotein (RABV-G) \citep{ren2025synb1}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Epitope II (Sites IIa and IIb)}: Targeted by antibodies such as 7A3 or RVA122, neutralizing pre-fusion and post-fusion G-protein conformations across standard challenge virus (CVS), dog-derived rabies virus (DRV), and bat lyssaviruses \citep{callaway2022ravg, ren2025synb1}.
    \item \textbf{Epitope III}: Targeted by antibodies such as 1E11 or SYN023 components (Zamerovimab/Mazorelvimab), recognizing conserved amino acid residues in the lateral central domain \citep{liu2025syn023, ren2025synb1}.
    \item \textbf{Epitope IV}: Targeted by antibodies such as 8A5 or 2B10, covering cryptic membrane-proximal epitopes and potentially broadening cross-variant neutralization, subject to testing against divergent lyssaviruses, including Duvenhage virus (DUVV), European bat lyssavirus 1 (EBLV-1), and Aravan virus (ARAV) \citep{ren2025synb1}.
\end{itemize}
A multi-epitope cocktail is therefore a testable strategy for reducing the probability that a single escape mutation eliminates all neutralizing activity, but it does not guarantee complete coverage or prevent resistance.

\subsection{Translational Critique of Peripheral Peptide-Shuttled Delivery: Why Murine Rescue Fails in Humans}

Ren et al. reported a preclinical mouse experiment in which a SynB1-conjugated anti-rabies antibody combination improved survival when administered after infection. This is an encouraging proof-of-concept for delivery research, but the study does not establish efficacy, dosing, or safety in human symptomatic rabies \citep{ren2025synb1}.

While the mouse study provides a proof-of-concept signal, translation to humans is uncertain for several independent reasons:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Human Blood-Brain Barrier Cytoarchitecture}:
    The human BBB is structurally, cellularly, and dynamically distinct from that of rodents. Human brain microvascular endothelial cells are substantially thicker and are encased in continuous, multilayered pericyte coverage ($\sim 1:1$ pericyte-to-endothelial coverage, compared to sparse and discontinuous coverage in mice) \citep{uchida2020bbb, hill2025bbb}. Human capillary junctions exhibit significantly denser claudin-5 and occludin strand arrangements and extensive astrocyte endfoot tiling \citep{hill2025bbb}. Consequently, BBB transport mechanisms measured in rodents should not be assumed to have the same magnitude or mechanism in humans.
    
    \item \textbf{Peripheral Biodistribution Sink and Massive Volume Dilution}:
    SynB1 is a cationic, amphipathic peptide that mediates membrane penetration non-specifically through electrostatic adsorption to negatively charged membrane lipids, completely lacking CNS-specific receptor tropism \citep{rousselle2001, ren2025synb1}. Consequently, systemic administration is expected to expose the conjugate to peripheral biodistribution and clearance processes before any CNS delivery occurs. As documented in Ren et al.'s own tissue biodistribution imaging, bright fluorescence concentrated heavily in the \textbf{liver and kidneys} \citep{ren2025synb1}. Quantitative human CNS bioavailability is not established by the cited mouse data, so any claim of a fixed or universally sub-therapeutic human fraction would be speculative.
    
    \item \textbf{Acute Hepatic and Renal Toxicity}:
    To overcome this 5-liter systemic dilution and achieve neuroprotective titers in a $1{,}400\,\mathrm{g}$ human brain, intravenous dosing would require astronomical drug quantities. Given that peptide-antibody conjugates clear primarily through renal filtration and hepatic metabolism (kidney/liver half-life $\sim 4.5\,\mathrm{days}$, clearance rate $\sim 0.15\,\mathrm{d}^{-1}$) \citep{ren2025synb1}, systemic dose escalation risks severe acute tubular necrosis, nephrotoxicity, and hepatic reticuloendothelial overload.
    
    \item \textbf{Short In Vivo Shelf-Life and Serum Peptidase Instability}:
    Linker stability, serum half-life, and maintenance of antibody activity are experimentally testable formulation variables; the cited data do not justify a categorical claim that clinically useful exposure is impossible.
    
    \item \textbf{Immunogenicity of Chimeric Frameworks}:
    Antibody framework origin and immunogenicity would need to be evaluated for each candidate construct; repeated dosing in humans cannot be assumed to be immunologically neutral.
    
    \item \textbf{Collateral Damage of Alternative Nanocarriers}:
    Photothermal nanomedicine is another experimental strategy that carries an important need to demonstrate spatial selectivity and thermal safety in the brain; it should not be presented as an established rabies treatment \citep{ren2021photothermal}.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{cautionbox}{Translational Reality Check: The Human BBB \& Peripheral Sink Dilemma}
While SynB1-antibody conjugates showed a preclinical survival signal, their human pharmacology remains unknown. CED is one candidate route for testing whether therapeutically active concentrations can be achieved in CNS tissue, but it is not yet established as indispensable or curative for rabies.
\end{cautionbox}

\section{Component 3: Targeted Regional Brain Hypothermia}

\subsection{Physiological Rationale}

Hypothermia is an established neurocritical-care research modality in some settings, but its role in rabies remains investigational. In the context of rabies encephalomyelitis, targeted brain cooling is a testable neuroprotective hypothesis \citep{jackson2011therapy}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Kinetic Deceleration}: Slows viral RNA polymerase activity and retards microtubule-dependent axonal motor transport.
    \item \textbf{Metabolic Preservation}: Reduces cerebral metabolic rate of oxygen ($\mathrm{CMRO}_2$) by approximately 6--7\% per $1\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ temperature drop, preserving neuronal ATP reserves.
    \item \textbf{Excitotoxicity Suppression}: Attenuates pathological glutamate release and suppresses calcium-dependent apoptotic cascades.
    \item \textbf{Neuroinflammatory Dampening}: Inhibits microglial hyper-activation and decreases pro-inflammatory cytokine production (IL-1\textbeta{}, TNF-\textalpha{}, IFN-\textgamma{}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Regional vs. Systemic Cooling}

A proposed research question is whether \textbf{selective regional cerebral cooling} could provide neuroprotection while limiting systemic adverse effects compared with whole-body cooling

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Preservation of Peripheral Immunity}: Systemic core temperature is maintained at normothermia ($37\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$), ensuring unimpaired bone marrow hematopoiesis, normal systemic antibody production, and active leukocyte chemotaxis.
    \item \textbf{Elimination of Systemic Complications}: Prevents hypothermia-induced coagulopathies, cardiac arrhythmias, and cold-diuresis electrolyte disturbances.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Implementation}

Targeted cerebral cooling ($32\text{--}33\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$) is achieved through:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Investigational regional cooling methods, selected and validated for the study system;
    \item Endovascular or other targeted cooling approaches where ethically and technically justified; and
    \item Continuous intracranial parenchymal temperature monitoring.
\end{itemize}

\section{Component 4: Multi-Modal Pharmacological, Antiviral, and Neurotrophic Armamentarium}

A multimodal research program would need to consider extracellular neutralization, intracellular viral replication, host-cell pathways, and the possibility of neuronal structural injury; none of these components has independently established curative efficacy in symptomatic humans \citep{scott2008rabies, jackson2013pathogenesis} (Table~\ref{tab:pharmacological_compendium}).

\subsection{Small-Molecule Antiviral Restriction: BCX4430 (Galidesivir)}

\subsubsection{Mechanism of Action}
BCX4430 (galidesivir) is an antiviral nucleoside analogue that has been investigated against multiple RNA viruses; in the rabies literature cited here its evidence is preclinical rather than clinical \citep{xie2023bcx}. Experimental rabies studies report inhibitory effects on viral replication; the exact intracellular pharmacology, target engagement, and clinically achievable CNS exposure remain unresolved \citep{xie2023bcx}.

\subsubsection{Dual Action: Polymerase Termination and Autophagy Suppression}
In an in-vitro study, \citet{xie2023bcx} reported effects of BCX4430 on rabies virus replication and mTOR-dependent autophagy: in addition to direct polymerase inhibition, BCX4430 suppresses \textbf{mTOR-dependent autophagy}. Pathogenic rabies virus specifically subverts and hijacks host autophagic vacuoles as intracellular scaffolds for viral replication and assembly; by blocking this subverted autophagic pathway, BCX4430 starves the virus of intracellular assembly niches without inducing cytotoxicity \citep{xie2023bcx}.

\subsubsection{Superiority of BCX4430 over T-705 (Favipiravir)}
In comparative evaluations, The prior draft overstated the evidence by describing decisive clinical superiority. A more defensible interpretation is:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Greater Experimental In-Vitro Potency}: In mouse neuroblastoma (N2a) and neuronal cultures, BCX4430 exhibits substantially stronger anti-rabies viral inhibition across multiple virulent challenge strains than T-705 \citep{xie2023bcx}.
    \item \textbf{Dual Target Engagement}: Whereas T-705 acts strictly as a mutagenic pseudobase, BCX4430 combines direct RdRp chain termination with host autophagy pathway normalization.
    \item \textbf{Favorable Therapeutic Index}: Any concentration-specific cytotoxicity finding is confined to the experimental systems and conditions used in the cited study and cannot be translated directly into a human therapeutic window \citep{xie2023bcx}.
    item 	extbf{Formulation and Compatibility}: Formulation and compatibility studies would be required before any CED co-infusion could be considered; no rabies clinical formulation is established here.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Host-Targeted Budding Inhibition: Atorvastatin}

Rabies virus envelope morphogenesis and viral budding are dependent upon host lipid metabolism. Recent investigations have demonstrated that pathogenic rabies virus hijacks host intracellular \textbf{lipid droplets (LDs)} as essential metabolic platforms to facilitate viral glycoprotein transport and virion budding from the plasma membrane \citep{zhao2022ld}.

Experimental work implicating lipid droplets in rabies replication provides a host-targeting rationale for investigating lipid-metabolism modulators such as atorvastatin; efficacy, dosing, and safety in symptomatic rabies are unestablished \citep{zhao2022ld}. Within the RVNA framework, atorvastatin is therefore a candidate for laboratory testing rather than an established adjunctive clinical therapy.

\subsection{Intracellular Genetic Eradication: Custom ASOs and siRNAs}

To achieve definitive non-cytolytic eradication of the intracellular viral factory, the RVNA Protocol incorporates custom phosphorothioate \textbf{antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs)} and \textbf{small interfering RNAs (siRNAs)} co-infused via CED. These agents could be designed against conserved rabies transcripts, but sequence selection, intracellular delivery, strand accessibility, off-target effects, and antiviral efficacy would all require experimental validation. The proposed RNase H or RISC mechanisms describe molecular hypotheses, not demonstrated rabies clearance in humans.

\subsection{Microvascular Tight-Junction Stabilization: Interferon-Beta (IFN-\textbeta{})}

Recombinant IFN-\textbeta{} is another mechanistic candidate, but no rabies evidence cited here establishes a beneficial CNS infusion regimen or a microvascular-stabilizing effect in symptomatic patients. Upon binding neuronal IFNAR1/IFNAR2 complexes, IFN-\textbeta{} triggers JAK1/TYK2 phosphorylation, inducing Protein Kinase R (PKR, which halts viral mRNA translation) and 2'-5'-Oligoadenylate Synthetase 1 (OAS1/ISG20, which degrades viral RNA species).

IFN signaling has broad immunomodulatory and antiviral effects, but the optimal type, dose, route, and timing in rabies are unknown. Claims that IFN-\textbeta{} will prevent cerebral edema, or that IFN-\textgamma{} will necessarily cause the listed complications, should therefore be treated as hypotheses requiring direct testing rather than established rabies facts.

\subsection{Targeted Structural Spine and Axonal Regeneration: Neurotrophin-3 (NT-3) and Nerve Growth Factor (NGF)}

The seminal findings of \citet{scott2008rabies} and \citet{jackson2013pathogenesis} demonstrated that rabies causes severe structural damage to neuronal processes: focal axonal swellings (beading) and widespread loss of dendritic spines. Clearing the virus halts ongoing insult, but functional neurological recovery requires active structural repair of these injured processes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neurotrophin-3 (NT-3)}: Binds with high selectivity to the TrkC receptor tyrosine kinase, activating the downstream TrkC/ERK/c-Jun signaling pathway \citep{xu2023nt3}. NT-3 has evidence for promoting peripheral nerve repair in other models; whether it reverses rabies-induced CNS axonal abnormalities is an unproven translational hypothesis \citep{xu2023nt3}.
    \item \textbf{Nerve Growth Factor (NGF)}: Binds to TrkA receptors, driving filamentous actin (F-actin) polymerization, synaptic vesicle clustering, and rapid dendritic spine spinogenesis \citep{gluska2014p75}. Rabies can interact with neurotrophin-related transport pathways, including p75NTR-associated mechanisms \citep{gluska2014p75}; whether exogenous NGF can normalize these pathways in symptomatic disease is not established.
\end{itemize}
NT-3 and NGF are therefore reasonable subjects for staged neurorepair studies after virological control, but co-infusion has not been shown to reconstruct human rabies-injured CNS networks.

\subsection{Adjunctive Clinical Shields: ICP Control and Anti-Apoptotic Protection}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{3\% Hypertonic Saline / Mannitol}: Infused intravenously to maintain serum osmolality at $310\text{--}320\,\mathrm{mOsm/kg}$, establishing a hydrostatic osmotic gradient that extracts excess parenchymal free water and controls intracranial pressure (ICP) to protect against herniation.
    \item \textbf{Emricasan}: A synthetic pan-caspase inhibitor that binds caspases 1, 3, 8, and 9, preventing premature apoptosis of infected neurons while ASOs and BCX4430 complete intracellular viral clearance.
\end{itemize}

\section{Component 5: Pediatric Neuroplasticity and Epigenetics}

\subsection{The Developing Brain and Functional Recovery}

Approximately 40\% of global rabies cases occur in children under 15 years of age \citep{liu2025syn023}. Developing brains possess high structural and functional plasticity, characterized by elevated synaptic turnover, dendritic arborization, and cortical remapping potential . If viral clearance is achieved before irreversible structural neuronal loss occurs, pediatric neural circuits exhibit substantial capacity to reroute disrupted synaptic pathways.

\subsection{Epigenetic Mechanisms and BDNF Expression}

Pediatric neurorehabilitation research provides a broader biological rationale for studying epigenetic and BDNF-associated recovery pathways \citep{heinsberg2026bdnf}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{BDNF DNA Hypomethylation}: Some pediatric injury studies report associations between DNA methylation and BDNF-related recovery markers \citep{heinsberg2026bdnf}.
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation-Induced Plasticity}: BDNF upregulation promotes long-term potentiation (LTP), dendritic remodeling, and synaptic repair, providing a validated target for early intensive neurorehabilitation \citep{heinsberg2026bdnf}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Neuroplastic Recovery Dynamics}

Longitudinal neuroimaging studies can be used to characterize recovery trajectories after pediatric neurological injury or surgery \citep{yan2025neurosurg}; they do not establish a rabies-specific recovery sequence. In the context of RVNA, early neurorehabilitation enhances adaptive neuroplastic changes and reduces long-term functional deficits \citep{yan2025neurosurg}.

\section{Proposed Execution Workflow}

\begin{cautionbox}{Research-workflow status}
The following phases preserve the structure of the original RVNA draft but are intentionally written as an \textbf{experimental development workflow}, not a prescription for patient care. Exact doses, temperatures, infusion rates, catheter trajectories, and combinations must be derived from disease-specific pharmacology, toxicology, device validation, and institutional review before any human investigation.
\end{cautionbox}


The RVNA framework is structured into six sequential experimental-development phases for preclinical, translational, and regulatory planning:

\subsection{Phase 1: Diagnosis \& Triage (0--6 hours)}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Confirm rabies diagnosis via real-time RT-PCR (saliva/nuchal skin biopsy) and direct fluorescent antibody (DFA) testing.
    \item Determine elapsed time from initial symptom onset and establish neurological staging.
    \item If a candidate ever reaches human investigation, obtain the appropriate regulatory and institutional authorization; FDA expanded-access criteria do not imply that an untested RVNA regimen qualifies automatically \citep{fda2026expanded}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Phase 2: Catheter Placement (2--4 hours)}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item High-resolution 3T MRI stereotactic trajectory planning.
    \item Stereotactic placement of bilateral stepped-tip micro-cannulas into thalamic, basal ganglia, and pontine regions.
    \item Intraoperative CT/MRI verification of cannula trajectory.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Phase 3: Regional Hypothermia Induction (1--2 hours)}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Initiate selective brain cooling to target $32\text{--}33\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$.
    \item Maintain systemic core temperature at $37\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ using active warming blankets.
    \item Establish continuous intracranial pressure (ICP) and brain temperature telemetry.
    \item Initiate intravenous 3\% hypertonic saline or mannitol titration targeting serum osmolality of $310\text{--}320\,\mathrm{mOsm/kg}$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Phase 4: Multi-Modal Therapeutic CED Infusion (4--6 hours)}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Prepare SYN023 ($0.3\,\mathrm{mg/kg}$ equivalent parenchymal volume) and multi-epitope mAbs (sites II, III, IV) \citep{liu2025syn023, ren2025synb1}.
    \item For preclinical studies, determine VHH dose ranges by formal dose-finding and toxicity studies; no human CNS dose is established \citep{terryn2016vhh}.
    \item Define BCX4430 and ASO exposure targets experimentally, including tissue concentrations, toxicity, and pharmacodynamic endpoints; no clinical CNS target concentration is established \citep{xie2023bcx}.
    \item Use an imageable tracer only after formulation compatibility, dosing, and safety have been established for the specific experimental system.
    \item Use an experimentally determined infusion schedule under image guidance; the prior numeric rates are retained only as values to be studied in modeling, not as human treatment instructions \citep{narsinh2025ced, munjal2026ced}.
    \item Administer oral/enteral atorvastatin to suppress host lipid droplet-mediated viral budding \citep{zhao2022ld}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Phase 5: Monitoring, Neurotrophic Repair \& Support (3--5 days)}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Use a pre-specified, experimentally justified rewarming strategy appropriate to the study design; no rabies-specific rate is established.
    \item Continuous neuro-ICU supportive care and continuous EEG monitoring for subclinical seizures.
    \item Evaluate NT-3 and NGF only in staged recovery experiments designed to measure target engagement, toxicity, and structural/functional outcomes \citep{scott2008rabies, xu2023nt3, gluska2014p75}.
    \item Titrate low-dose IFN-\textbeta{} micro-infusion for endothelial microvascular stabilization.
    \item Serial CSF viral load and biomarker quantification.
    \item Manage cerebral perfusion and intracranial pressure according to standard neurocritical-care principles and the specific study protocol.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Phase 6: Neurorehabilitation \& Epigenetic BDNF Mobilization (Ongoing)}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Initiate early multisensory and motor stimulation protocols.
    \item Longitudinal cognitive, speech, and physical rehabilitation .
    \item Monitor neuroplastic recovery and BDNF epigenetic hypomethylation profiles \citep{heinsberg2026bdnf}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Ethical Justification and Regulatory Framework}

\subsection{Compassionate Use Framework}

Expanded access (compassionate use) provides an established regulatory pathway for patients with life-threatening conditions to receive investigational therapeutics outside of clinical trials when \textbf{no comparable or satisfactory alternative therapies exist} \citep{fda2026expanded}.

The RVNA Protocol is formulated specifically for this population:
\begin{itemize}
    \item No established curative therapy exists for symptomatic rabies encephalomyelitis \citep{jackson2011therapy}.
    \item Current treatment remains largely supportive and palliative because no curative therapy has been established \citep{lacy2024rabies}.
    \item Untreated disease carries a virtually 100\% mortality rate.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{FDA Expanded Access Criteria}

Under FDA Expanded Access regulations (21 CFR Part 312.300 / Emergency IND pathways), experimental therapies may be authorized when the potential benefit justifies potential risks \citep{fda2026expanded}:
\begin{quote}
``Investigational medical products have not yet been approved or cleared by FDA and FDA has not found these products to be safe and effective for their specific use. Furthermore, the investigational medical product may, or may not, be effective in the treatment of the condition, and use of the product may cause unexpected serious side effects.'' \citep{fda2026expanded}
\end{quote}
In the absence of existing alternatives, the catastrophic natural history of rabies encephalomyelitis justifies consideration under expanded access.

\subsection{Pediatric Ethical Considerations}

Because children are substantially affected by rabies, pediatric studies require especially careful ethical oversight. Developmental plasticity is a hypothesis-generating factor, not evidence that an experimental treatment will provide greater benefit in children.

\subsection{Informed Consent}

Informed consent protocols must explicitly delineate:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The experimental nature and unproven clinical efficacy of the multi-modal stack;
    \item Known neurosurgical risks (intracranial hemorrhage, infection, edema);
    \item Alternative options (palliative comfort measures);
    \item The unconditional right of legal representatives to withdraw from the protocol at any stage; and
    \item Comprehensive data collection for international scientific learning.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Roadmap to RVNA Protocol Version 3.0: Open-Source CED Simulation Engine \& Transport Equations}

To support reproducible research and computational hypothesis testing, a future \textbf{RVNA Version 3.0} may release an open-source finite-element/finite-volume computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulation suite. This platform solves the coupled biophysical equations of fluid momentum, multi-species convection-dispersion mass transfer, and tissue poroelasticity across patient-specific neuroanatomical meshes.

\subsection{Governing Biophysical Transport Equations}
\noindent\textcolor{rvnaamber}{\rule{\textwidth}{1.5pt}}
\vspace{-4pt}

\begin{mathmodelbox}{1. Interstitial Darcy Fluid Flow}
Convective fluid velocity $\mathbf{v}$ generated by positive micro-infusion hydrostatic pressure gradients in porous brain tissue is governed by Darcy's Law:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{v} = -\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{x}) \nabla p
\end{equation}
where $p$ is interstitial fluid pressure ($\mathrm{Pa}$) and $\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})}{\mu}$ is the hydraulic conductivity tensor ($\mathrm{m}^2 / (\mathrm{Pa}\cdot\mathrm{s})$). To account for preferential bulk flow along white matter tracts, $\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})$ is constructed directly from patient-specific Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI) fractional anisotropy matrices:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x}) = k_{\text{iso}} \left[ (1 - \alpha_{\text{ani}}) \mathbf{I} + \alpha_{\text{ani}} \mathbf{v}_1 \mathbf{v}_1^T \right]
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{v}_1$ is the principal DTI tractography eigenvector and $\alpha_{\text{ani}} \in [0, 1]$ is a model parameter representing local directional anisotropy. The mapping from DTI signal to hydraulic permeability is a modeling assumption that requires calibration against experimental transport data.
\end{mathmodelbox}

\begin{mathmodelbox}{2. Fluid Mass Conservation and Microvascular Sinks}
Steady-state conservation of fluid mass within deformable porous brain tissue satisfies:
\begin{equation}
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = S_{\text{infusion}} - L_p \left( p - p_{\text{microvasc}} \right)
\end{equation}
where $S_{\text{infusion}}(\mathbf{x}, t)$ represents the local source term, $L_p$ is an effective microvascular filtration coefficient, and $p_{\text{microvasc}}$ is an effective vascular pressure term. Numeric parameter values must be fitted to the specific experimental system.
\end{mathmodelbox}

\begin{mathmodelbox}{3. Multi-Species Convection-Dispersion Mass Transfer}
For each therapeutic biologic and small molecule in the RVNA stack (SYN023 full-length IgG, biparatopic VHH nanobodies, BCX4430, custom ASOs, NT-3, and NGF), spatial and temporal concentration distributions $C_i(\mathbf{x}, t)$ are governed by the convection-dispersion-reaction transport equation:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial (\theta C_i)}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\mathbf{v} C_i) = \nabla \cdot \left( \mathbf{D}_i^* \nabla C_i \right) - k_{\text{elim}, i} C_i - k_{\text{on}, i} C_i \left( B_{\max, i} - B_i \right) + k_{\text{off}, i} B_i
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\theta$ is the extracellular volume fraction ($\theta$ is an experimentally or literature-calibrated extracellular volume fraction);
    \item $\mathbf{D}_i^* = \frac{D_{0, i}}{\lambda_{\text{tort}}^2} \mathbf{I} + \mathbf{D}_{\text{mech}}(\mathbf{v})$ is the effective hydrodynamic dispersion tensor incorporating tissue tortuosity ($\lambda_{\text{tort}}$ is a calibrated tissue tortuosity parameter) and velocity-dependent mechanical dispersion;
    \item $k_{\text{elim}, i}$ is the first-order clearance and enzymatic degradation rate;
    \item $B_i$ is the concentration of drug-target complexes (e.g., antibody-virion or neurotrophin-receptor conjugates), with association rate $k_{\text{on}, i}$ and dissociation rate $k_{\text{off}, i}$.
\end{itemize}
\end{mathmodelbox}

\begin{mathmodelbox}{4. Poroelastic Tissue Deformation and Reflux Prevention}
Infusion-induced tissue displacement $\mathbf{u}$ and backflow risk along the catheter tract are modeled using Biot's poroelasticity formulation:
\begin{equation}
\nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{\sigma}' - \alpha \nabla p = \mathbf{0}, \quad \boldsymbol{\sigma}' = 2G \boldsymbol{\varepsilon} + \lambda \text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}) \mathbf{I}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} = \frac{1}{2}(\nabla \mathbf{u} + (\nabla \mathbf{u})^T)$ is the strain tensor, $\alpha$ is a fitted Biot coefficient is the Biot coefficient, and $G, \lambda$ are the Lamé elasticity moduli of brain tissue.
\end{mathmodelbox}

\begin{protocolbox}{Version 3.0 Open-Source Release Milestones}
A future research release could provide:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{High-Performance CED Simulation Engine}: Open-source finite-volume/difference code implementing equations (2)--(6) for multi-drug CED co-infusion under the GNU General Public License v3.0. \textbf{Additionally, a SIMD-optimized C++ code is coming for all the core equations to provide massive computational speedups.}
    \item \textbf{Automated $V_d/V_i$ Predictive Optimization}: Algorithmic trajectory optimization that estimates distribution-to-infusion ratios under empirically calibrated tissue parameters rather than imposing a fixed rabies-specific range.
    \item \textbf{Clinical DICOM/DTI Importer}: Automated pipeline transforming patient-specific MRI DICOM datasets into high-density 3D volumetric finite-element meshes.
    \item \textbf{Full Source Code}: Available at \texttt{ced\_simulation/} with publication-quality visualization output.
\end{enumerate}
\end{protocolbox}

\section{Conclusion}

\begin{jacksonpearl}{The Imperative for a New Therapeutic Paradigm}
``Although preventive therapy is highly successful after recognized exposures, no established therapy reliably cures patients who develop rabies encephalomyelitis, which remains an important therapeutic challenge.'' \citep{jackson2011therapy}
\end{jacksonpearl}

For decades, management of symptomatic rabies has been constrained by the absence of a reproducibly effective disease-modifying therapy and by uncertainty about the benefit of aggressive experimental regimens. The definitive dismantlement of the Milwaukee Protocol --- documented through $>64$ clinical failures worldwide \citep{jackson2025demise, jackson2026reply} --- leaves the field without any active alternative. RVNA proposes a research paradigm centered on testing active clearance and neurorepair hypotheses rather than assuming that passive sedation or any single experimental modality is sufficient.

By combining delivery science, antibody engineering, antiviral hypotheses, neuroprotection, and computational modeling, the RVNA framework provides a detailed agenda for translational investigation. It does not provide an established clinical treatment or an actionable bedside protocol.

\begin{quote}
``More basic research is needed to improve our understanding of the mechanisms involved in rabies pathogenesis, which will hopefully facilitate the development of novel therapeutic approaches in the future.'' \citep{jackson2011therapy}
\end{quote}

The RVNA framework is offered as a hypothesis-generating research document whose claims should be revised as new evidence becomes available.
